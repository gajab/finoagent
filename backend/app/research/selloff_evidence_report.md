# Selloffs Forecast Volatility Far Better Than Direction

The most robust finding in credible research on the 1–20 sessions after a sharp S&P 500 selloff is that **volatility stays high and the distribution of outcomes widens**. The direction of the next move is only weakly and conditionally predictable. Since about 2000 the index has shown mild short-horizon reversal: the S&P 500's weighted 5-day autocorrelation flipped from **+0.050 to −0.141**. That reversal is strongest when volatility and volume are high. But rigorous tests find that the VIX level has no reliable 1–3-month return power on its own, and that moving-average timing, volatility timing and "buy the dip" rules lose much or all of their edge out-of-sample. For the Selloff Monitor, the largest gains therefore come less from new signals than from statistical honesty. With k = 40 analogs, the current Wilson test can only detect edges of roughly 15 percentage points. The analogs cluster in a handful of crises, the library blends a 1990s regime that tended to continue with a post-2000 regime that tends to reverse, and the engine has never been scored walk-forward against the base rate. Several components also rest on conventions rather than evidence. Signed Cboe data show SPX market makers net *long* gamma in 88% of intervals, which contradicts the dealers-long-calls/short-puts sign the monitor assumes. HY OAS lags equity. Flights to safety mostly end within three sessions. The breadth triggers have no peer-reviewed out-of-sample record and were calibrated on NYSE data, not S&P 500 members. The best-supported upgrades are listed in priority order in the last section.

## Since 2000 the index mildly reverses, but only volatility forecasts survive out-of-sample

**The baseline flipped sign.** Across 20 major indexes, daily serial dependence hovered near +0.05 from 1951, declined from the 1980s, and has been negative since the 2000s. For the S&P 500, the weighted multi-day autocorrelation MAC(5) moved from **+0.050 before March 1999 to −0.141 after** (t-difference −4.30). The break is dated to August 1987, and SPY has averaged −0.087 since its 1993 launch ([Baltussen, van Bekkum & Da](https://academicweb.nd.edu/~zda/Indexing.pdf)). The authors tie the flip to index futures and ETFs rather than a time trend: a 1-point rise in indexing lowers MAC(5) by about 0.031. Trading against the autocorrelation earned a Sharpe ratio of 0.67 on the S&P 500, though the authors doubt most investors could capture it after rebalancing costs.

Part of the earlier positive autocorrelation was an artifact of stale cash-index prices: futures on the same indexes showed near-zero autocorrelation ([Ahn et al.](https://www.nber.org/papers/w7214)). This is why older and newer event studies disagree. Across 39 markets in 1989–1998, prices **continued** in the direction of large shocks for up to about 10 days, and that effect was already "clearly weaker in the late 1990s" ([Lasfer, Melnik & Thomas](https://cronfa.swansea.ac.uk/Record/cronfa2231)). Later market-level work finds reversal after −1% days ([Dolvin & Pyles](https://butler.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/daily-stock-returns-momentum-reversal-or-both/)). The monitor's library starts in 1990, after the S&P 500's estimated break, so the contamination is milder than for a 1950s-start library. But the daily sign did not fully switch until the 2000s, so roughly a quarter of the library sits in a transition regime that leans toward continuation.

**Reversal concentrates in exactly the states where the monitor gets consulted.** A century of daily US data shows autocorrelation that is **positive at low volatility and negative at high volatility**, and more negative after declines ([Sentana & Wadhwani](https://ideas.repec.org/a/ecj/econjl/v102y1992i411p415-25.html)). LeBaron independently finds serial correlation inversely related to volatility ([LeBaron](https://ideas.repec.org/a/ucp/jnlbus/v65y1992i2p199-219.html)). Campbell, Grossman & Wang show that **a price decline on heavy volume is more likely to be followed by a rise in expected return** than the same decline on light volume, because liquidity providers demand compensation for absorbing selling that carries no information ([Campbell, Grossman & Wang](https://dash.harvard.edu/bitstream/1/3128710/2/campbell_trading.pdf)). Nagel finds that short-term reversal returns, his proxy for the return to liquidity provision, "rise sharply when the VIX is high." Industry reversal strategies that earn little on average earn high returns in turmoil ([Nagel](https://www.nber.org/papers/w17653.pdf)). Daniel & Moskowitz show that the violent rebounds of 1932 and 2009 arrived in "panic" states: a market that had declined, combined with high volatility ([Daniel & Moskowitz](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w20439/w20439.pdf)).

Together these support a conditional statement. A sharp, high-volume decline in a high-volatility, post-2000 market is more likely than average to see a partial bounce over 1–5 sessions. None of these papers tests a 3-session N-σ trigger out-of-sample, and the research found no peer-reviewed study that does.

**The conditional mean is the fragile part.** In monthly DJIA data from 1900 to 2009, predictability varies over time, and **during market crashes no statistically significant predictability was found** ([Kim, Shamsuddin & Lim](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/empfin/v18y2011i5p868-879.html)). The VIX level is the most popular "capitulation" gauge, yet by itself it "has no predictive power for future one- and three-month returns" ([Johnson 2011 draft](https://www.bauer.uh.edu/departments/finance/documents/seminars/Johnson_020712.pdf)), and "the squared VIX itself fails to predict stock returns" ([Bekaert & Hoerova](https://www.nber.org/papers/w18995.pdf)).

An in-sample computation on Yahoo Finance closes for 1990–2026, done for this report, reproduces the descriptive "buy extreme fear" pattern Giot reported ([Giot](https://ideas.repec.org/p/cor/louvrp/1848.html); data: [^VIX](https://finance.yahoo.com/quote/%5EVIX/), [^GSPC](https://finance.yahoo.com/quote/%5EGSPC/)). The mean 20-day forward log return was **+2.13% when VIX was 30–40, versus +0.57% below 15**. After 5-day drops of −5% or worse (46 episodes), the index averaged +1.15% over the next 5 days and +2.5% over the next 20. But VIX ≥ 40 covers only **13 independent episodes**, the spread of outcomes widens as much as the mean rises, and no out-of-sample test exists.

The same computation separates the speed of a decline from its depth. After a drawdown of 10% or more from the 252-day high, the 20-day mean was only **+0.34%**. A fast washout and a grinding bear market are different states even when they register the same σ. The reference point matters too. Nearness of the Dow to its 52-week high predicts *higher* future market returns, while nearness to its all-time high predicts *lower* ones ([Li & Yu](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/356820)). That evidence is at monthly horizons, and because the two anchors point in opposite directions, a 52-week drawdown feature alone merges two different states.

**Trend state carries information, mostly about risk.** Index technical indicators, including long moving averages, forecast the monthly equity premium both in- and out-of-sample ([Neely, Rapach, Tu & Zhou](https://files.stlouisfed.org/files/htdocs/wp/2010/2010-008.pdf)). The gains concentrate in bad times: a practitioner summary reports a utility gain of 0.89% in expansions versus 14.9% in recessions for the longest MA rule ([CXO summary](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/fundamental-valuation/technical-boost-to-fundamental-stock-market-forecasting)). Goulding, Harvey & Mazzoleni classify markets as Bull, Correction (slow trend up, fast trend down), Bear or Rebound, and find that corrections and rebounds carry predictive information. Their dynamic rule cut the worst drawdown to −13.2% from −18.8% over 1990–2022, though turning points became more frequent after the global financial crisis ([Goulding, Harvey & Mazzoleni](https://people.duke.edu/~charvey/Research/Published_Papers/P167_Breaking_bad_trends.pdf)).

Against this, Sullivan, Timmermann & White ran a reality check across thousands of rules. Out-of-sample in 1987–1996, the best rules earned a **statistically insignificant 8.63% a year** before costs ([Sullivan, Timmermann & White](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/119144); [FMG dp303](https://www.fmg.ac.uk/sites/default/files/2020-11/dp303.pdf)). Time-series momentum persists over 1–12 months and partially reverses beyond that ([Moskowitz, Ooi & Pedersen](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2089463)). That places the monitor's 20-session horizon between the reversal regime (1–5 days) and the trend regime (months), which is the horizon where its claims deserve the least confidence.

**The volatility forecast is what survives out-of-sample.** Negative returns raise next-period variance more than positive returns of the same size; this is the GJR leverage term ([GJR, cited via arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.02695)). Patton & Sheppard show that future volatility tracks the **realized semivariance of past negative returns**, that negative jumps raise subsequent volatility and positive jumps lower it, and that models exploiting this forecast significantly better out-of-sample from one day to three months ahead ([Patton & Sheppard](https://public.econ.duke.edu/~ap172/Patton_Sheppard_Realized_Semivariance_7oct11.pdf)).

Expected returns do not keep pace with volatility. Volatility-managed portfolios raise Sharpe ratios precisely "because changes in volatility are not offset by proportional changes in expected returns" ([Moreira & Muir](https://www.nber.org/papers/22208)). Even that finding failed the harder test: across 103 strategies, feasible out-of-sample volatility-managed versions earned **lower certainty-equivalent returns and Sharpe ratios** than unmanaged ones, which the authors blame on unstable scaling coefficients ([Cederburg et al.](https://www.lehigh.edu/~xuy219/research/COWY.pdf)). The lesson for a selloff monitor is that it can confidently forecast a wider, more left-skewed distribution, and much less confidently forecast where the distribution is centred. A win rate identical to the base rate can hide a far fatter left tail.

## The options cone runs a quarter too wide in calm markets and too narrow after crashes

**Risk-neutral densities are biased forecasts of real outcomes, and the bias can be corrected.** Bliss & Panigirtzoglou find that raw option-implied densities fail forecast tests, while densities adjusted with a power-utility risk aversion cannot be rejected as unbiased ([Bliss & Panigirtzoglou](https://www.chicagofed.org/~/media/publications/working-papers/2001/wp2001-15r3-pdf.pdf)). A secondary summary puts the implied relative risk aversion near 8 at a one-week horizon, falling to about 2 at six weeks ([arXiv summary](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1910.14522)). Shackleton, Taylor & Yu report that risk-transformed S&P 500 option densities nearly always pass calibration tests at 1-, 2- and 4-week horizons, more often than historical or GARCH alternatives ([Shackleton, Taylor & Yu](https://www.efmaefm.org/0EFMAMEETINGS/EFMA%20ANNUAL%20MEETINGS/2006-Madrid/papers/627120_full.pdf)). Ross-recovery methods fail, and simple power-utility or historical benchmarks "cannot be rejected" ([Jackwerth & Menner](https://www.aeaweb.org/conference/2018/preliminary/paper/yNn4GdY9)). So a simple, deterministic map from the risk-neutral (Q) distribution to the real-world (P) distribution is both legitimate and better than the raw cone, and sophisticated recovery adds nothing.

**An in-sample calibration check shows how large the bias is and how much it depends on regime.** The table below tests a VIX-scaled lognormal cone over 1990–2026 ([^VIX](https://finance.yahoo.com/quote/%5EVIX/), [^GSPC](https://finance.yahoo.com/quote/%5EGSPC/)). Forward 21-day realized volatility exceeded VIX on only **14.7% of days**. That is consistent with a 35-year CFA Institute analysis showing mean VIX of 19.59 against forward realized volatility of 15.50 ([CFA Institute](https://blogs.cfainstitute.org/blog/2024/07/31/how-well-does-the-market-predict-volatility)).

| Condition | Horizon | Outside ±1σ (nominal 31.7%) | Below P10 (nominal 10%) | Above P90 (nominal 10%) | SD of z (nominal 1.0) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| All days | 1 day | 21.7% | 6.6% | 6.2% | 0.86 |
| All days | 5 days | 17.0% | 4.8% | 3.2% | 0.77 |
| All days | 20 days | 15.4% | 4.3% | 2.8% | 0.77 |
| VIX ≥ 40 | 1 day | 38.5% | — | — | 1.16 |
| VIX ≥ 40 | 20 days | 19.7% | — | — | 0.73 |
| After 5-day drop ≤ −5% | 1 day | **43.6%** | 12.7% | **18.8%** | 1.26 |
| After 5-day drop ≤ −5% | 5 days | 30.4% | 6.1% | 10.5% | — |
| After drawdown ≤ −10% | 20 days | 20.0% | **7.4%** | 3.0% | — |

Three patterns matter. In calm and normal regimes the cone is roughly a quarter too wide. Immediately after a sharp drop the 1-day cone is too narrow, and the misses are mostly on the **upside** (18.8% above P90), reflecting rebound volatility. After a deep drawdown, the 20-day misses swing back to the **downside** (7.4% below P10 versus 3.0% above P90), reflecting cascade risk. Forward realized volatility exceeded VIX on 27.1% of days after a −5% week and on 23.1% of days when VIX was at or above 40, and the median ratio of realized volatility to VIX rose from 0.73 to 0.84.

**These figures use VIX as a proxy, and the monitor's straddle cone is narrower.** An ATM straddle prices roughly the risk-neutral mean absolute move, and with negative skew ATM implied volatility sits below the variance-swap rate. The over-width above is therefore an upper bound for the straddle cone, whose own correction factor stays unknown until the monitor logs its history. The straddle-to-σ mapping, σ√T ≈ straddle·√(π/2)/S, rests on the Brenner–Subrahmanyam approximation for at-the-money-forward options ([Gundersen](https://www.gregorygundersen.com/blog/2023/08/19/atm-approximation/)). A short derivation shows its P10 and P90 are robust to tail shape: a normal's 10/90 quantiles sit at 1.606 mean absolute deviations and a Laplace's at 1.609. Its P1 and P99 are not robust (2.92 versus 3.91 mean absolute deviations), so the cone should not be extended past P10/P90 without information from the option wings.

**The variance risk premium (VRP) is the best-documented options-based return predictor, but at the wrong horizon for this monitor.** Bollerslev, Tauchen & Zhou's VIX²-minus-realized measure explained **more than 15% of quarterly excess-return variation** in 1990–2005 ([BTZ, Fed working paper](https://www.federalreserve.gov/Pubs/Feds/2007/200711/200711pap.pdf)). Replacing past realized variance with a better HAR forecast leaves only the quarterly horizon significant and cuts R² from about 7% to 3–5%. The authors conclude that "results in BTZ exaggerate the predictive power," and adjusted R² is "small at the one month horizon" ([Bekaert & Hoerova](https://www.nber.org/papers/w18995.pdf)). Pyun calls BTZ's out-of-sample performance "comparably weak" and needs a beta-conditioned variant to reach roughly 8% monthly out-of-sample R² ([Pyun](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jfinec/v132y2019i1p150-174.html)). For a 1–20 session tool, the VRP's best use is sizing the Q→P adjustment, not calling direction.

**The VIX term structure behaves the same way.** Its slope forecasts returns on variance swaps, VIX futures and straddles, which is the price of variance risk, rather than expected changes in VIX ([Johnson 2017](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-financial-and-quantitative-analysis/article/abs/risk-premia-and-the-vix-term-structure/56572D1F060448571BD8F597C732D9C3)). For equity returns, the full term structure adds an insignificant 1.99% of R² at one month and 5.16% at three months. That power comes from an obscure fourth principal component, and inversion is largely a proxy for a high VIX level ([Johnson 2011 draft](https://www.bauer.uh.edu/departments/finance/documents/seminars/Johnson_020712.pdf)). The VIX-futures basis does not forecast spot VIX changes ([Simon & Campasano](https://www.efmaefm.org/0EFMAMEETINGS/EFMA%20ANNUAL%20MEETINGS/2013-Reading/papers/EFMA2013_0164_fullpaper.pdf)), and VIX-futures premiums fall when risk rises ([Cheng](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v32y2019i1p180-227..html)).

The monitor's "VIX ≥ VIX3M" rule therefore reads a regime, not a signal, and in-sample data show that regime has two very different outcomes. Since 2006, mild backwardation (VIX/VIX3M of 1.00–1.10) preceded above-average 20-day returns: +1.60% for ratios of 1.00–1.05, and +4.00% for 1.05–1.10 (93 days). **Above 1.10, the mean was −2.26%, the median +1.27% and the 10th percentile −21.3%** across 18 episodes, and forward realized volatility ran 18% above VIX ([^VIX3M](https://finance.yahoo.com/quote/%5EVIX3M/)). Within VIX ≥ 25, inverted days averaged +1.02% over the next 20 days versus +1.98% for non-inverted days, so inversion added no bullish information beyond the VIX level. Extreme backwardation is a snap-back-or-cascade state, and the right display is a wider, more left-heavy fan, not a call.

**Tail measures mostly price insurance; they do not time crashes.** High VVIX coincides with expensive tail hedges, which then earn lower returns over the next 3–4 weeks ([Park, Fed FEDS 2013-54](https://www.federalreserve.gov/pubs/feds/2013/201354/201354abs.html)). Industry sources describe the Cboe SKEW index as pricing tails rather than predicting them, with a level that has drifted from about 115 in the 1990s to the 140s–150s ([LuxAlgo](https://www.luxalgo.com/library/concept/skew-index/)). The research found no peer-reviewed evidence that SKEW times crashes. A downside-only decomposition of SKEW does predict downturns up to a year ahead out-of-sample ([Bevilacqua & Tunaru](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/108198)). Skew and variance premia share a single factor; the skew premium accounts for more than 40% of the slope of the implied-volatility curve ([Kozhan, Neuberger & Schneider](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v26y2013i9p2174-2203.html)), so a composite that counts both is voting twice on the same information. Cross-sectional tail risk predicts *higher* market returns, +4.5% over the next year per standard deviation ([Kelly & Jiang](https://www.nber.org/papers/w19375.pdf)), which is far outside a 1–20 session window.

## Credit confirms late, flights to safety fade within three sessions, and the grid's thresholds are conventions

**Equity leads credit.** In weekly firm-level data, lagged stock returns explain bond-yield changes but not the reverse ([Kwan](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jfinec/v40y1996i1p63-80.html)). Stock returns lead CDS and bond spreads, and that sensitivity rises as credit quality worsens ([Norden & Weber](https://madoc.bib.uni-mannheim.de/1879/)). Hourly stock returns predict HY and BBB bond returns ([Downing, Underwood & Xing](https://ideas.repec.org/a/cup/jfinqa/v44y2009i05p1081-1102_99.html)). Equity leads CDS at daily and weekly frequency, with no flow in the other direction ([Hilscher, Pollet & Wilson](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-financial-and-quantitative-analysis/article/abs/are-credit-default-swaps-a-sideshow-evidence-that-information-flows-from-equity-to-cds-markets/F7CCD2FFB20AAEACE1E093D12EEF7A31)). Daily stock returns lead HY but not IG bond returns ([Tolikas](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/eurjfi/v24y2018i10p849-866.html)). The exception is the HY CDS index: there causality runs both ways, and CDS gains the edge in adverse markets ([Procasky](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/finmar/v54y2021ics1386418120300501.html)).

This matters for the monitor's rule that HY OAS must widen by at least 15 bp over the same 3-session window as SPY. A fast equity selloff can sit in "equity-only de-risking" for a day or two purely because cash credit, and the evaluated-price OAS series on FRED, have not caught up. Credit "not yet confirming" on day one or two is weak evidence of calm. A fixed basis-point threshold also means different things at 280 bp and at 600 bp of spread.

**The excess bond premium (EBP) supplies the right idea at the wrong frequency.** Gilchrist & Zakrajšek split credit spreads into two parts: a default component explained by equity-based distance to default, which accounts for about 70% of bond-level spread variation, and a residual premium. A **+50 bp rise in the EBP raises the 12-month recession probability by about 15 percentage points, versus 7 points for the same rise in the raw spread**. The default component adds nothing, and the worst false positive was the 2002 accounting-scandal spike ([Gilchrist & Zakrajšek, AER](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.102.4.1692); [Favara et al., FEDS Note](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econresdata/notes/feds-notes/2016/recession-risk-and-the-excess-bond-premium-20160408.html)). Tighter bank credit standards likewise predict lower stock returns, but at business-cycle frequency ([Chava, Gallmeyer & Park](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/moneco/v74y2015icp117-132.html)). No peer-reviewed study tests HY OAS levels or changes as predictors of S&P 500 returns over 1–20 days.

What transfers is the decomposition. Credit that widens only as much as the equity drop implies is an echo of equity. Credit that widens beyond that is new information. Bond ETFs are timelier than OAS: in March 2020 some HY and IG ETFs traded at 6–10% discounts to NAV because NAVs lagged illiquid cash markets ([IOSCO](https://www.iosco.org/library/pubdocs/pdf/IOSCOPD682.pdf); [BIS Bulletin 6](https://www.bis.org/publ/bisbull06.pdf)). But HYG carries interest-rate duration, so in a 2022-style rates selloff it falls without any credit stress at all.

**Flights to safety are acute and short.** Baele, Bekaert, Inghelbrecht & Wei define a flight to safety (FTS) as a day with a large equity loss, a large bond gain, negative high-frequency stock–bond correlation and high equity volatility. They find FTS on **fewer than 2% of days**, with **62% of spells lasting one day, about 94% lasting three days or fewer, and none longer than 10** ([Baele et al.](https://business.columbia.edu/sites/default/files-efs/citation_file_upload/Flights%20to%20Safety.pdf)). Stock–bond correlation on these days averages −58.5%; the yen gains 0.44% and the Swiss franc 0.33%. The US TED spread rises only 5 bp, so "FTS episodes … are not always a credit event." The authors describe FTS as producing "steep, though short lived, spikes in expected equity returns."

They also give a test that can run in real time. Equity must be down, bonds up, and the joint bivariate-normal tail probability must fall below a κ·σ threshold, with κ = 1.26 calibrated to a 4.66% incidence. Volatilities and correlation come from a 360-day window that excludes the last 5 days. The monitor's TLT ±0.3% rule points the right way but uses a raw percentage. And requiring credit confirmation for "broad risk-off" will miss many genuine FTS days.

When stocks and bonds fall *together*, the regime is different. Positive stock–bond correlation is most common under high inflation and high real rates, and it comes with higher 60/40 portfolio volatility ([Molenaar et al.](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/research/financial-analysts-journal/2024/empirical-evidence-on-the-stock-bond-correlation)). In 2022 the S&P 500 fell 18.1% and long Treasuries 26.1% ([Alpha Architect, secondary](https://alphaarchitect.com/stock-bond-correlation/)). High implied volatility makes negative stock–bond correlation over the following month several times more likely ([Connolly, Stivers & Sun](https://ideas.repec.org/a/cup/jfinqa/v40y2005i01p161-194_00.html)).

**Yen strength alone is not a carry unwind.** Carry-trade returns are negatively skewed. Unwinds cluster when the VIX rises and funding liquidity falls, and carry losses reduce future crash risk while raising its price ([Brunnermeier, Nagel & Pedersen](https://www.nber.org/papers/w14473)). In August 2024 the TOPIX fell 12% in one day as leveraged equity and FX positions unwound; the yen carry trade was estimated at roughly ¥40 trillion, and "markets then stabilised quickly" ([BIS Bulletin 90](https://www.bis.org/publ/bisbull90.htm)). Because the yen also rises on ordinary FTS days, the monitor's USD/JPY −1.5% rule should require VIX stress and Japanese equity weakness as well before applying a carry label. No peer-reviewed study measures S&P 500 returns over the 1–20 days after a carry unwind.

**Official stress indices predict the macroeconomy, not next month's S&P 500.** The Chicago Fed's NFCI aggregates 105 measures and helps forecast GDP 2–4 quarters ahead ([Chicago Fed](https://www.chicagofed.org/-/media/publications/nfci/nfci-faqs-pdf.pdf)). Worse financial conditions widen the left tail of GDP growth about a year out ([Adrian, Boyarchenko & Giannone](https://www.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/aer.20161923)). The OFR's stress index is daily but published with about a two-day lag ([OFR](https://www.financialresearch.gov/financial-stress-index/)). A low-quality practitioner test found that weekly changes in the St. Louis Fed index had no power for next-week S&P returns ([Seeking Alpha](https://seekingalpha.com/article/427561-financial-stress-indicator-a-guide-to-market-returns)).

Their construction, however, is the best available template. The ECB's CISS index converts each indicator to a rank on its own expanding history, averages within five market segments, and combines the segments using time-varying correlations (an exponentially weighted average with λ = 0.93 on weekly data, about 0.986 daily by our conversion). That way stress counts for more when it hits several segments at once. Weighting segments by real-economy impact or equally produced paths that were "not very large" apart. A threshold model found that stress shocks below a CISS level of 0.3233 had no significant effect on output ([Holló, Kremer & Lo Duca](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/pub/pdf/scpwps/ecbwp1426.pdf)).

The monitor's z-scores against the prior 60 days measure shocks, not states. After a calm stretch, a small σ inflates the z-score; inside a stressed regime, large moves get muted. No peer-reviewed study shows that any composite stress index improves 1–20 day S&P forecasts. Any composite the monitor builds must therefore earn its place in the same walk-forward test as the analogs.

## Breadth describes the damage, and momentum crashes arrive on the rebound

**The peer-reviewed case for breadth as a forecaster is thin, and it points toward continuation rather than contrarian bounces.** Across 64 countries over 1973–2018, high-breadth market and industry portfolios outperform low-breadth ones at monthly horizons, even after controlling for trend signals ([Zaremba et al.](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/ecmode/v97y2021icp348-364.html)). Breadth-type sentiment measures, including advance/decline, move with the market but show little power for weekly or monthly returns ([Brown & Cliff, via CXO](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/sentiment-indicators/does-investor-sentiment-give-trading-signals)).

The monitor's specific triggers have no independent out-of-sample test. The Zweig thrust's claimed record is about **20 NYSE signals since 1945**, averaging roughly +23% over the following year. On a broader universe, 6 thrusts since 2010 averaged +14.9%, a sample the source itself flags as highly uncertain. Signals in November 2023 and April 2025 fired on NYSE data but peaked at **0.612** on the broader universe, just short of the 0.615 trigger ([thetrading.tools](https://www.thetrading.tools/concepts/zweig-breadth-thrust)). A 95% Wilson interval on 6 successes out of 6 runs from about 61% to 100% (our computation).

Lowry's 90% days were defined on NYSE volume and points. The signal is a 90% up day *after* a cluster of 90% down days, not a single panic day; the 1973–74 bear market had 15 such down days ([Ritholtz Q&A with Desmond](https://ritholtz.com/2011/02/qa-paul-desmond-of-lowrys-part-ii)). Practitioner studies of extreme %-above-moving-average readings are mixed and short ([Schaeffer's](https://www.schaeffersresearch.com/content/analysis/2021/04/21/can-the-s-p-500-index-keep-up-its-impressive-run)). The same Goldman Sachs strategy team called narrow breadth a warning in 2020 and not a worry in 2018 ([ai-CIO](https://www.ai-cio.com/news/bad-breadth-5-giants-market-dominance-grim-sign-goldman-says/); [Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2018-07-30/dearth-of-depth-in-u-s-stock-rally-isn-t-scaring-goldman-sachs)). Computed on S&P 500 members rather than the roughly 3,000 NYSE issues they were calibrated on, these thresholds are different indicators.

**Historical breadth computed from today's constituents is biased in a known direction.** Stocks added to the index run up before joining: one study found about 77% abnormal returns in the three years before inclusion ([Morningstar](https://global.morningstar.com/en-gb/funds/sp-500-bump-that-doesnt-last); [NY Fed SR 484](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr484.html)). Stocks that collapsed and were deleted are missing. Look-back statistics therefore overstate past % above moving averages and new highs, and understate past new lows, most severely in market-wide washouts. Today's reading is unbiased, but any percentile, 52-week window or Zweig moving-average history is not. The monitor's existing refusal to show breadth percentiles is correct; the fix for history is point-in-time index membership ([QuantConnect forum](https://www.quantconnect.com/forum/discussion/21226/would-you-pay-for-cheap-point-in-time-s-amp-p-500-data-survivorship-bias-free/)).

**The strongest evidence in this area concerns the momentum factor, and it runs opposite to the intuition behind a crowded-unwind label.** Daniel & Moskowitz show that momentum crashes can be forecast from an ex-ante panic state: a negative 24-month market return plus high 126-day variance. The crashes happen **when the index rebounds**. In July–August 1932 the market rose 82% while past losers rose 232%. In March–May 2009 the market rose 26% and losers 163%. The winners-minus-losers portfolio lost 74.4% in August 1932, a month in which the market gained 36.5% ([Daniel & Moskowitz](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w20439/w20439.pdf)).

Scaling momentum exposure by its own recent variance "virtually eliminates crashes and nearly doubles the Sharpe ratio" ([Barroso & Santa-Clara](https://ciencia.ucp.pt/en/publications/momentum-has-its-moments/)). Comomentum is the abnormal return correlation among past winners and losers. High comomentum predicts momentum returns 1.06% a month lower in year one, and raises the share of "bad" momentum days in the following three months from 8.4% to 22.5% ([Lou & Polk](https://personal.lse.ac.uk/polk/research/hhab117.pdf)). Holdings-based crowding is contested. Hedge funds with more crowding exposure suffered larger drawdowns in industry distress ([Brown, Howard & Lundblad](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v35y2022i7p3231-3271..html)), but institutional-holdings crowding proxies were *negatively* related to crash risk ([Barroso, Edelen & Karehnke](https://ideas.repec.org/a/cup/jfinqa/v57y2022i4p1313-1342_3.html)).

**The monitor's "Crowded-leadership unwind" label describes something else: de-grossing in a falling tape.** The rule is SPY down, a leadership spread at or below −1.5σ, and RSP beating SPY. For that pattern, the index-level evidence is essentially anecdotal.

The 2007 quant quake is the forensic benchmark. A market-neutral contrarian strategy lost 6.85% over August 7–9, a 7.6σ move, then rebounded 5.92% on August 10, leaving the week at −0.43% ([Khandani & Lo](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/conference/2007/liquidity/Khandani_Lo.pdf)). The damage was front-loaded and partly reversed within a day, but that is one episode. July 2024 went the other way. After the July 11 CPI print, the Russell 2000 rose 11.5% while the Nasdaq-100 fell about 5% over five sessions, and the S&P 500 lost only about 1%. The index then slid to an early-August low roughly 10% below its peak as the yen carry trade unwound ([CME Group](https://www.cmegroup.com/openmarkets/equity-index/2024/Behind-the-Sector-Rotation-Away-from-Tech.html)). On DeepSeek day the S&P 500 was about −1.9% intraday while the Dow rose ([Reuters via Tiger](https://www.itiger.com/hans/news/2506845515)). On November 9, 2020, momentum lost nearly 5% in one day in a rallying market ([MSCI](https://www.msci.com/research-and-insights/blog-post/are-momentum-wings-finally-starting-to-melt)).

Concentration likewise explains how the index moves, not where it goes next. When the top 10 stocks held 30% or more of the market, the other 490 beat them in 84% of the following 5-year windows ([Hartford Funds](https://www.hartfordfunds.com/insights/market-perspectives/strategic-beta-etfs/the-messy-aftermath-of-concentrated-markets.html)). But stepping aside at the late-2020 concentration record would have missed years of gains ([Morningstar](https://www.morningstar.com/stocks/stock-market-concentration-has-surpassed-its-1930s-peak-should-investors-worry)). The monitor's 60% "mega-cap driven" cut-off is an attribution convention, not a forecast.

## Dealer gamma is a volatility proxy whose sign the monitor assumes

**The monitor's dealers-long-calls/short-puts convention has exactly one peer-reviewed validation, and it covers a narrow use.** Baltussen, Da, Lammers & Martens build the same convention-signed gamma from public SPX open interest; their OptionMetrics series matched SqueezeMetrics almost exactly over 2002–2017. In S&P 500 futures, when the prior day's gamma is negative, the last 30 minutes continue the rest of the day's move with a slope of **0.0663 (t = 4.78)**. When gamma is positive the slope is **0.0082 (t = 1.03)**, indistinguishable from zero ([Baltussen et al.](https://www3.nd.edu/~zda/intramom.pdf)). Pooled across equity indexes, the effect has an out-of-sample R² of 2.88%, and it **reverts over the following days**. A −2% move by 3:30 pm under negative gamma implies roughly 13 bp more decline into the close: meaningful for execution, small next to daily volatility. Nothing in the paper validates a gamma-flip price, gamma walls or a next-day direction call.

**Signed data contradict the convention's usual sign.** Using Cboe volume broken out by trading capacity, Adams et al. find SPX market-maker net gamma **positive in 88% of 10-minute intervals, and positive on average in every month of 2018–2024** ([Adams et al.](https://www.jean-sebastienfontaine.com/papers/0dte-options-volatility.pdf)). Customers start each day short at-the-money 0DTE straddles and condors, so market makers are long gamma on both puts and calls near spot. The effects are dampening and small. A one-standard-deviation rise in 0DTE market-maker gamma lowers next-interval volatility by 23 bp annualized, and 0DTE expiration days run 61 bp (7%) lower.

Using proprietary Cboe trade records, Amaya et al. find market-maker gamma turns negative intraday on at least 25% of days, and on at least half of days after May 2022. Even so, the counterfactual effect on daily volatility has a median of −0.08 points and a worst case of **+3.3 volatility points**, against a 4.5-point standard deviation of daily volatility changes from all causes ([Amaya et al.](https://cdn.cboe.com/resources/education/research_publications/gammasqueezes.pdf)). In single stocks, where market makers are net long gamma on average, a one-standard-deviation gamma shock moves next-day absolute returns by 37 bp, about 12% ([Ni, Pearson, Poteshman & White draft](https://ou.edu/dam/price/Finance/CFS/paper/pdf/pearsonPoteshmanWhite.pdf)). Convention-based gamma, by contrast, was negative on 2,930 of 6,088 days in Baltussen's sample.

**The largest error sits in the gamma that matters most.** The convention books dealers short every put. It therefore assigns large negative gamma to near-the-money, short-dated puts that signed data show dealers actually own. That biases GEX downward near spot and pushes the computed flip toward or above the current price, which is exactly the condition that produces a "dealers short gamma" banner during selloffs.

SPY adds more uncertainty. Retail traders account for more than 60% of US options volume and favour cheap weekly contracts ([Bryzgalova, Pavlova & Sikorskaya](https://ideas.repec.org/p/cpr/ceprdp/17688.html)). Retail call buying would leave dealers short calls, the opposite of the convention. No public signed position data exist for SPY, and SPY covers only part of total S&P 500 option gamma alongside SPX and E-mini options.

SqueezeMetrics' marketing paper reports a 1-day SPX standard deviation of 0.55% in the top GEX quartile versus 0.85% in the next ([SqueezeMetrics](https://slopeofhope.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/11/GEX-white_paper.pdf)). But convention GEX rises mechanically in calm rallies as call gamma grows, so part of that gap is just the volatility regime under another name. Open interest is less of a problem than the sign. Positions that become 0DTE add about 25 bp of hedging need on expiration day, versus 3 bp from new same-day trades ([Adams et al.](https://www.jean-sebastienfontaine.com/papers/0dte-options-volatility.pdf)), so yesterday's open interest captures most of what matters.

**Pinning is real where settlement is physical and the hedge is the underlying.** Optionable stocks cluster at strike prices at expiration, shifting returns by at least 16.5 bp ([Ni, Pearson & Poteshman 2005](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=519044)). S&P 500 futures pin on serial futures-option expirations, but the **index basket shows no pinning from SPX expirations, and futures show anti-pinning** ([Golez & Jackwerth](https://d-nb.info/1112655492/34)). The post-opex "unclench," vanna and charm flows, and flip-level support and resistance appear only in vendor commentary ([SpotGamma](https://spotgamma.com/gamma-exposure-explained/); [FlashAlpha](https://flashalpha.com/articles/why-gex-isnt-enough-vanna-charm-exposure-predict-market-moves)).

## Forty analogs can detect only a fifteen-point edge

**Analog forecasting works in meteorology under conditions this engine does not yet meet.** Weather forecasters use long data libraries, cut the number of dimensions hard, and calibrate outputs against climatology. In finance the track record is modest. Nearest-neighbour predictors on the Nikkei 225 lost to a random walk on point accuracy while beating it on direction, mostly in bear markets ([Fernández-Rodríguez et al.](https://accedacris.ulpgc.es/handle/10553/49193?mode=full)). Lo, Mamaysky & Wang used the monitor's own design, comparing the distribution of returns after a pattern with the unconditional distribution, and found incremental information for some patterns ([Lo, Mamaysky & Wang](https://www.nber.org/papers/w7613)).

Weather analog ensembles show high reliability when built and verified properly ([Delle Monache et al.](https://opensky.library.ucar.edu/islandora/object/articles%3A12922)). Analogs drawn from historical reforecasts beat raw ensemble probabilities, but only after explicit calibration on a long, consistent archive ([Hamill & Whitaker, via NOAA WPC](https://www.wpc.ncep.noaa.gov/research/model_qpf_files/Page643.htm)). Van den Dool estimated that hemisphere-wide weather analogs accurate to observation error would need a library on the order of 10^30 years ([NOAA CPC](https://cpc.ncep.noaa.gov/products/people/wd51hd/sst/readme.html); [arXiv, secondary](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.04530)). That is why the field reduces dimensions before it searches for analogs.

**The current significance rule is statistically sound but has almost no power.** The monitor flags a result when the all-days baseline falls outside the 95% Wilson interval. That is exactly a two-sided score test that treats the baseline as known, and Wilson is the recommended small-sample interval ([Brown, Cai & DasGupta](https://projecteuclid.org/euclid.ss/1009213286)). But with n = 40 and a baseline near 62%, "significant" requires at least 31 or at most 18 up outcomes: an edge of roughly +15 or −17 points. A true 10-point edge is detected only **28% of the time**, and 80% power for a 10-point edge needs about 176 independent analogs (our computation). Most real post-selloff edges are a few points. The honest wording for a non-significant result is "not distinguishable from the base rate," not "no edge."

**Non-overlap fixes only part of the dependence problem.** The non-overlap rule removes mechanical overlap among the 40 forward windows. It does not remove episode clustering: 40 analogs can come from eight crises. Nor does it fix the nesting of the 1-, 5- and 20-session statistics, which come from the same paths, so agreement across horizons is close to automatic ([Boudoukh, Richardson & Whitelaw](https://www.nber.org/papers/w11841)). The all-days baseline uses overlapping 20-session windows. Under a Gaussian approximation, that inflates the variance of its hit rate by about 14.6 times (3.8 times at 5 sessions) (our computation, following [Hansen & Hodrick](https://doi.org/10.1086/260910) and [Newey & West](https://doi.org/10.2307/1913610)). For a 40-member fan, the 95% interval on the 10th percentile stretches from the sample minimum to the 9th-smallest value.

**Multiple comparisons compound the problem.** Each refresh tests roughly seven statistics against baselines. If they were independent and every null hypothesis true, at least one false "significant" flag would appear about **30% of the time** (our computation), and the monitor refreshes daily. The design choices themselves (five features, a 1.5 weight, k = 40, a 20-session horizon) were made by people who know how 2008, 2020 and 2022 unfolded, and no on-screen p-value can correct for that. The literature's answers are reality-check tests across configurations ([White](https://doi.org/10.1111/1468-0262.00152); [Hansen SPA](https://ideas.repec.org/p/wyi/wpaper/002018.html); [Romano & Wolf](https://econ-papers.upf.edu/papers/712.pdf)), a higher bar of t > 3 in heavily mined settings ([Harvey, Liu & Zhu](https://www.nber.org/papers/w20592)), and deflating results by the number of trials ([Bailey & López de Prado](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2460551)).

**The distance metric has three fixable defects**, based on a read of `find_analogs` in `backend/app/services/selloff_monitor_service.py`. First, the 52-week drawdown, the 200-day distance and log VIX largely measure a single stress factor. With per-feature scaling, that factor carries roughly triple weight relative to the two σ-scaled move features; a whitened (Mahalanobis) distance fixes this. Second, the feature weight multiplies *squared* differences, so the 1.5 weight on the 3-session move stretches that axis by √1.5 ≈ 1.22, not 1.5. Third, feature scales come from a full-sample standard deviation. That is harmless for the live forecast but leaks future information into any backtest.

At five dimensions, distance concentration is not yet the binding problem; it can set in by 10–15 dimensions, when nearest and farthest neighbours become hard to tell apart ([Beyer et al.](https://minds.wisconsin.edu/handle/1793/60174)). Adding features carelessly would make it one. The engine also always returns 40 analogs, even for a state with no real precedent. And in a record selloff every neighbour is calmer than today, which pulls the forecast toward milder outcomes.

**The single most important fix is a walk-forward scorecard.** Out-of-sample, the usual equity-premium predictors fail to beat the historical mean ([Welch & Goyal](https://nber.org/papers/w10483)). Modest restrictions, such as sign constraints and shrinkage toward the mean, recover small but economically meaningful skill ([Campbell & Thompson](https://nber.org/papers/w11468)). Shrinking many noisy proportions toward a common mean reduces total prediction error ([Efron & Morris](https://doi.org/10.1080/01621459.1975.10479864)).

For the monitor, that means displaying a shrunk estimate rather than the raw analog hit rate. The formula is p̃ = (k + m·p₀)/(n + m), where k is the number of up outcomes among n analogs, p₀ is the base rate, and m is a prior strength tuned to minimise walk-forward Brier score. For example, 30 up out of 40 against a 62% base rate becomes 68.5% at m = 40 (our computation).

Skill should be measured with strictly proper scoring rules against a base rate computed only from data available at each date. The Brier score, with Murphy's split into reliability and resolution, suits up/down probabilities and path events; the CRPS suits the full fan ([Gneiting & Raftery](https://doi.org/10.1198/016214506000001437); [Siegert](https://ore.exeter.ac.uk/rest/bitstreams/141507/retrieve)). Calibration should be checked with PIT histograms ([Diebold, Gunther & Tay](https://doi.org/10.2307/2527342)) and reliability diagrams with consistency bars ([Bröcker & Smith](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/22217)). Intervals should come from block bootstraps or Diebold–Mariano tests, because serial correlation makes naive intervals too narrow ([Wilks](https://doi.org/10.1002/qj.709); [Diebold & Mariano](https://doi.org/10.1080/07350015.1995.10524599)). Expect wide uncertainty. The Brier skill score is biased, rare events need a few hundred forecast–outcome pairs before skill shows ([Bradley, Schwartz & Hashino](https://doi.org/10.1175/2007WAF2007049.1)), and stress origins since 2000 supply perhaps 100–300 non-overlapping episodes.

## Upgrades ranked by evidence and payoff

Evidence grades are defined as follows. **Strong** means peer-reviewed, long samples, and replicated or validated out-of-sample. **Moderate** means peer-reviewed but a single study, no clean out-of-sample test, or a horizon that does not match 1–20 sessions. **Weak** means practitioner backtests, small or in-sample evidence, or established construction practice with no predictive validation. **Folklore** means no independent test was found. The ranking weighs how wrong or overconfident the current output is, how strong the evidence is, and how much the upgrade costs to build. A Strong grade on a methodological upgrade means the statistical method is well established, not that the monitor will gain forecasting skill from it.

| Current component | Role the evidence supports | Evidence for 1–20 session index prediction |
|---|---|---|
| k-NN analogs (5 features, k = 40) | A tilt on the base rate | Unvalidated; nearest-neighbour evidence in finance is directional only and modest |
| ATM-straddle cone | Risk-neutral range | Moderate; too wide in calm regimes, too narrow at 1 day after crashes |
| Cross-asset grid and rule diagnosis | Describes how broad the stress is | Weak; thresholds are conventions, and credit lags equity |
| Breadth (Zweig, 90% days, % above MA) | Describes participation | Weak to folklore; calibrated on NYSE, not S&P 500 members |
| Factor anatomy, crowded-unwind label | Describes what was sold | Strong for the factor's own crash risk; none for the index |
| SPY dealer gamma | Volatility and autocorrelation regime | Moderate for intraday continuation only; sign uncertain |

## Conclusion

The evidence points to a different job for the monitor. Asking "will it bounce?" is the question the literature answers least reliably. "How wide, how skewed, and how sure are we?" is the question it answers best. The post-selloff state is often two-sided: a high VIX raises the mean and the spread together, and extreme backwardation pairs a positive median with a negative mean and a −21% tenth percentile. In that setting a single win rate or average actively misleads, while medians, both tails, forward realized volatility and first-passage odds carry the real information. What is most likely wrong with the monitor today is not a missing signal. It is confidence the sample cannot support, and labels (gamma sign, credit confirmation, crowded unwind, breadth thrusts) that present conventions as findings.

Several of the most decision-relevant numbers do not exist in the literature and can only come from the monitor itself. These include the right real-world scaling for a straddle cone, how often the conventional GEX sign matches realized hedging behaviour, the index's path after crowded-long de-grossing days, and breadth-conditioned outcomes on point-in-time membership. The cheapest high-value step is therefore to start logging now: daily snapshots of cone quantiles, GEX under every sign convention, each label that fired, and the outcomes that followed. Every month of logs turns a convention into evidence. Even "strong" effects have moved within the sample: autocorrelation changed sign around 2000, trend turning points multiplied after 2009, and dealer gamma dynamics changed with daily expiries after 2022. That makes the walk-forward skill badge the monitor's regime-change alarm as well as an honesty device.
